In plain words: what is this course about?

For someone who has never taken statistics or written code. Four ideas are enough to understand the course.

What you will learn

To answer medical questions with real data. For example: which patients in an intensive care unit are at the highest risk of dying? Is maternal anemia associated with birth weight? Statistics is the toolbox for answering those questions with numbers rather than impressions. Machine learning is its most recent extension: programs that learn patterns from thousands of cases to predict what will happen with a new patient.

What programming means here

Writing short instructions in Python, a programming language widely used in science, so the computer sorts, counts, plots, and analyzes the data for you. You do not need to know it beforehand: you learn it from the first class, with an artificial-intelligence assistant that helps you write and fix the code.

What Google Colab is

A free Google web page where you write and run code from the browser, much like a Google Docs document. Nothing is installed: you open a link and the class notebook is ready to use. All you need is a Google account and a computer with internet access.

What an open dataset is

A collection of real data (anonymized medical records, national health surveys, death registries) that hospitals and institutions publish for free so anyone can do research with it. The course works with the same datasets researchers use, not invented examples.

You will work with the same data researchers use

Every week you practice on real, open datasets — the same ones behind papers in international journals — instead of simplified teaching examples.

MIMIC-IV

Intensive-care medical records from a Harvard hospital. The course uses the open demo version and guides you through the process for full access.

PPMI

A longitudinal study of Parkinson's disease with clinical data, biomarkers, and neuroimaging; free access upon request.

NHANES

The U.S. national health and nutrition survey, with physical exams and laboratory data.

ENDES & SINADEF

The Peruvian datasets: maternal and child health (INEI) and the national death registry.

Global Burden of Disease

Disease burden by country, age, and sex, from IHME.

The weekly method

A short prep video before each class

Following the flipped-classroom model, a brief video introduces the intuition of the topic before the session, without unnecessary formalism, so live time is spent applying rather than lecturing.

Live class on Sundays at 8:00 p.m.

For two hours the concept is developed through medical examples and applied in a Google Colab notebook that every student runs and modifies in parallel. All sessions are recorded.

A weekly challenge in pods

Each pod brings together 4–6 students who work as a team throughout the course. From week 11 on, the pod develops its final project with the instructor's mentorship.

AI-assisted programming

From week 1 the course uses AI assistants to write and debug code, with an emphasis on learning to verify their output. It includes a demonstration of Claude Code automating a complete analysis. The course principle: the assistant collaborates on the code; the interpretation belongs to the student.

By the end of the course you will be able to

Use Python in Google Colab to import, clean, describe, and plot health data.
Interpret distributions, confidence intervals, p-values, and statistical power.
Choose and run the statistical tests most frequently used in medical research.
Fit and interpret linear and logistic regressions, handling confounders.
Train and evaluate machine-learning models and explain how neural networks work.
Complete a project on an open dataset and present it conference-style.

The 16 weeks

Includes an optional, self-paced module 0 (notation, logarithms and odds, matrices as data tables, basic probability), available from the moment you enroll.

Weeks 1–3 · Exploring and describing data
First steps in Colab and Python; variable types and a study's Table 1; principles of a good figure. Practice with NHANES, ENDES, and the Global Burden of Disease.
Weeks 4–6 · Probability, estimation, and the p-value
Distributions and the central limit theorem; confidence intervals and the bootstrap; what a p-value means (and what it does not); statistical power and sample size.
Weeks 7–8 · Statistical tests and linear regression
Student's t, chi-square, paired tests, and multiple comparisons; correlation and linear regression. Mini-project 1 is due.
Weeks 9–11 · Logistic regression and the leap to machine learning
Odds ratios and clinical interpretation; confounders and causal graphs; bias and variance, cross-validation, and the first predictive model with the MIMIC-IV demo.
Weeks 12–14 · Applied machine learning
Decision trees and random forests; gradient boosting and metrics (ROC, calibration); neural networks by intuition. Practice with MIMIC-IV and PPMI.
Weeks 15–16 · The final project
Project workshop in pods with the instructor's guidance, and conference-style final presentations. The closing covers the path toward publication and student conferences.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need experience in programming or statistics?

No. The course starts from zero and the optional module 0 covers the fundamentals before week 1. All work happens in Google Colab, in the browser, with nothing to install.

What is Google Colab? Do I have to install anything?

Google Colab is a free Google web page where you write and run code from the browser, like an online document. Nothing is installed: you open the class notebook link and it is ready to use. You only need a Google account.

What does “machine learning” mean?

Programs that learn patterns from many cases in order to predict an outcome in new ones; for example, estimating a patient's mortality risk from admission data. In the course you train and evaluate these models on real datasets, and learn to judge when they are useful in healthcare and when they are not.

What if I cannot attend a session?

Classes are recorded. Passing requires live participation in at least 12 of the 16 weeks, since pod work happens during the session.

How does the monthly contribution work?

It is a symbolic contribution: an equivalent course costs US$ 500 or more. The contribution is made at the start of each month of the course — S/ 150 via Plin from Peru, or US$ 49 by card from abroad — and it is required to participate. Stopping the contribution means losing access to classes and to the pod from that month on. A one-time contribution is also available: S/ 540 or US$ 179.

Can I pay from outside Peru?

Yes. From outside Peru you can pay by credit or debit card, with no account needed: monthly US$ 49 or full course US$ 179 (processed by PayPal, with its buyer protections). After paying, write to me on WhatsApp with your name to confirm your enrollment.

Why ask for a contribution if the project is non-profit?

Because the course needs to sustain itself: the symbolic contribution covers the platform, the recordings, the mentorship, and the scholarships, and lets me set aside the time the course requires. It is not meant to generate profit; it is meant to keep the project alive and to make sure price never leaves a student out.

Is there a certificate?

Yes. A digital certificate records the 32 hours of live class, the asynchronous work hours, and the title of your final project. It is granted upon passing with 60 % of the total score and presenting the project.

Is the course taught in Spanish?

Yes. Classes, guides, and the enrollment form are in Spanish; some preparation videos are in English with subtitles. You will need working Spanish to follow the course.

Is it open to people who are not medical students?

Yes. The course is aimed at medical and health-sciences students, and interested health professionals are also welcome.
